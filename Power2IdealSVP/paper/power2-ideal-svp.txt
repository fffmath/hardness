1     Coefficient-infinity SVP on principal power-of-two cyclotomic
      ideals
Theorem 1. For the fixed ring family Rn = Z[X]/(X n + 1), with n ranging over powers of two,
the following problem is NP-complete under deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions:
given f ∈ Rn \ {0} and an integer r ≥ 1, decide whether λ1,∞ (f Rn ) ≤ r, where the norm is the
coefficient infinity norm. The input lists the n integer coefficients of f and encodes r in binary.
    The argument below is purely theoretical. The dimension grows along the predefined power-
of-two family; it does not assert hardness in one constant numerical dimension. No conclusion for
coefficient Euclidean norm, canonical infinity norm, or constant-factor approximation is inferred
from this theorem. The reduction has an additive gap: YES instances have λ1,∞ ≤ r, while NO
instances have λ1,∞ ≥ r + 2. The radius can have exponentially large numerical value, so this
does not yield a constant-factor approximation gap.

Lemma 1 (Difference-clique extraction). If an integer set P has at least three elements and
every positive pair difference of P belongs to D, then its size is at most v and its elements have
the form
                                t + ε{ai : i ∈ C},    ε ∈ {−1, 1},
for a clique C of Γ.
Proof. Translate one element of P to zero. Every nonzero remaining element uniquely has
the form ai − aj , with ij an edge. For two distinct such elements ai − aj and ak − al , their
difference is ap − aq for an edge pq. Three-term uniqueness therefore gives {i, l, q} = {j, k, p}
as multisets. If i 6= k, then i 6= j implies i = p, whence {l, q} = {j, k}. Since k 6= l, this forces
l = j. Thus any two representing ordered pairs share their first or their second coordinate. A
family of at least two distinct pairs with this property lies in a single row or a single column:
if two share their first coordinate but have distinct second coordinates, every other pair must
share that first coordinate; the column case is symmetric. Their other indices, together with
the common index, form a source clique, by uniqueness of ordered differences. This gives the
asserted representation.

1.3   The forced support geometry
Lemma 3 (Exactly one trimer). A support satisfying the preceding field inequalities consists
of dimers (runs of length two) and exactly one trimer (a run of length three). All runs lie in
one short integer interval. After cutting at the long empty circular gap, let the trimer start at
t. Every dimer to its left starts at t + Qq and every dimer to its right at t + Qq + 1, for some
integer q. The set of these integers q, together with 0 for the trimer, has every positive pair
difference in D.

Proof. The forbidden guard makes the skew fields zero, so all active fields reduce to those of
−C(X + X −1 ) + T . Adjacent active signs are opposite. A support point with no adjacent
support neighbor therefore has field at most RT < τ , and cannot occur. Since distance three is
forbidden, every run has length two or three. A one-zero gap would put the last point of one run
at distance three from the second point of the next; a two-zero gap would put the two endpoints
at distance three. Thus gaps have at least three zeros.
      Every pair of support points has circular distance at most R0 . To see that they lie in a
short interval, move one to zero and represent the others in [−R0 , R0 ]. The difference of the two
extreme representatives is at most 2R0 < n − R0 , so its circular distance can be at most R0 only
if that difference itself is at most R0 . Thus the entire support lies in an integer interval of length
at most R0 . The complementary circular gap has length at least n−R0 , giving a consistent linear
ordering. Multiplication by a monomial X k is a signed cyclic permutation which commutes with
every multiplier. It preserves each local field under the induced permutation: if a coordinate
changes sign, both factors in σi (Gσ)i change sign. We may therefore place the entire short arc in
[0, R0 ]. All interactions between support positions can now be computed on this integer interval,
without a negative-cyclic boundary crossing.
      Two distinct trimers, whose start difference is δ ≥ 6, would supply all five distances δ −
2, . . . , δ + 2. They are larger than two and hence must lie in far windows. Such windows have
length four and are separated by at least Q − 3 > 1, so they cannot contain five consecutive
distances. There is at most one trimer; odd support size forces exactly one.
      For a left dimer with start p < t, its differences to the trimer are the four consecutive values
(t − p) + {−1, 0, 1, 2}. These fit an allowed far window only if t − p = Qd for some d ∈ D. For a
right dimer with start p > t, those four values are (p − t) + {−2, −1, 0, 1}, forcing p − t = Qd + 1.
Thus the asserted start positions follow. For two dimers on the same side, their three pairwise
distance values are Q|q − q 0 | + {−1, 0, 1}; these lie in a window only if |q − q 0 | ∈ D. For two on
opposite sides the values are Q(q 0 − q) + {0, 1, 2}, with the same conclusion. This proves the last
assertion.

   Conversely, take any source clique, select one label for the trimer, place the other components
according to the start rules in the lemma, and make the remaining components dimers. All
component differences then fit the prescribed windows. Their separations are at least Q − 2,
and they fit within an interval of length at most QA0 + 3 < n/2. This constructs an admissible
odd support with exactly the desired number of components.

    All integers have polynomial encoding length. The labels are polynomially bounded, n
is polynomial in the source size, and the largest guard coefficient 5s−1 has O(n) bits. All
subsequent sums, products, and norm bounds retain polynomial bit length. Ring generators
and their negacyclic multiplication bases are therefore constructible in polynomial time. Finally,
membership in NP follows by providing a nonzero integral coefficient vector v with |vi | ≤ r, whose
encoding has O(n log(r + 1)) bits. The multiplication matrix mf is nonsingular; rational linear
algebra computes m−1 f v and checks that its entries are integral. This verifies ideal membership
in polynomial time and completes the proof of the theorem.

1.6   A concrete obstruction to reusing the layer at finite exponents
The failure to infer finite-p hardness has a direct short-vector explanation. Keep f = (M − K)S,
RK = kKk1 , and M = 3RK + 1. For every fixed real 1 ≤ p < ∞ and n ≥ 4p , every full sign
vector satisfies

                 k(M − K)skp ≥ (M − RK )n1/p ≥ 4(M − RK ) > 2(M + RK ).

Here convolution has operator norm at most RK on coefficient `p . In contrast, 2 ∈ SRn gives
the nonzero ideal vector

                      2(M − K) ∈ f Rn ,       k2(M − K)kp ≤ 2(M + RK ).

Thus no image of the full sign layer can be a finite-p shortest vector in these dimensions. In
particular, for p = 2 the obstruction already applies when n ≥ 16. This rules out the direct
reuse of this specific minimum-layer argument; it does not give an algorithm for arbitrary ideals
or rule out a different finite-p reduction.
