                                             Abstract
      Let q be an odd prime. Let ζq be a primitive qth root of unity, and put K = Q(ζq ), with
  ring of integers OK = Z[ζq ]. As q ranges over odd primes, we prove that the decision version of
  the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) in the canonical ℓ2 -norm is NP-complete on full-rank free
                    2
  submodules of OK     by giving a deterministic polynomial-time many-one reduction from Exact
  Cover by 3-Sets (X3C). The module rank is fixed at two. The main obstacle is closure under the
  action of OK . A module containing a nonzero vector also contains every scalar multiple of that
  vector by a nonzero element of OK , and some of these multiples may be shorter.
      Three ideas overcome this obstacle. First, we map the Bennett–Peikert Reed–Solomon lattice
  to a principal cyclotomic ideal and use Wan’s point-count estimates to prove that a coset of this
  ideal contains many binary coefficient representatives. Second, we construct a checker whose
  canonical squared norm encodes the X3C equations, with a controlled nonnegative error term.
  Third, the checker and a second module coordinate combine with a separation bound for ideal
  cosets to rule out short vectors in NO instances. Each constructed instance consists of an odd
  prime q, two integral generators whose 2 × 2 generator matrix has nonzero determinant, and
  an integer squared threshold. The construction also gives NP-hardness of search-SVP under
  deterministic polynomial-time Turing reductions with at most one oracle call.
1    Introduction
A lattice L ⊆ Rm is the set of all integer linear combinations of linearly independent vectors. Given
a basis of L, the decision shortest vector problem (SVP) asks whether the lattice contains a nonzero
vector of squared norm at most a given threshold. Its search version asks for a shortest nonzero
lattice vector. In 1981, Van Emde Boas asked whether exact SVP is NP-hard [vEB81]. Later,
Ajtai’s breakthrough result established that exact Euclidean SVP is NP-hard under randomized
reductions [Ajt98]. For Euclidean SVP on lattices of rank n, later randomized reductions established
hardness of √ approximation within a factor of 1 + n−ε for every fixed ε > 0 [CN98], within any fixed
factor in (1, 2) [Mic01], and within every constant factor [Kho05, Mic12]. Stronger randomized-time
assumptions yield superconstant and almost-polynomial factors [Kho05, HR12].
    However, a deterministic NP-hardness proof for exact Euclidean SVP remained open for decades.
Along one route, Hair and Sahai used probabilistically checkable proofs (PCPs) to obtain deter-
ministic hardness for ℓp -norms with p > 2 [HS26b]. They also proved approximation hardness in
the ℓp -norm for every fixed 1 ≤ p < ∞ under a subexponential-time assumption [HS26a]. In two
recent preprints, Wan proved deterministic hardness for every constant approximation
                                                                               √               factor in
the Euclidean norm [Wan26a], extending an earlier result for factors below 2, including the exact
problem [Wan26c].
    At an intuitive level, Micciancio’s local-density program asks for an explicit lattice L and a coset
t + L containing many points of norm strictly below λ1 (L) [Mic01, Mic12]. The minimum of L
protects the soundness gap, while the abundance of short coset points allows the reduction to encode
a solution to the input problem. For our deterministic reduction, these points must realize every
prescribed assignment on a designated set of coordinates. Bennett and Peikert proposed a candidate
by lifting Reed–Solomon codes [BP23]. Wan used finite-field point counts to prove that an explicit
Reed–Solomon coset has the required representatives with prescribed coordinates [Wan26c]. Wan’s
result supplies the local-density statement needed for deterministic hardness results for general
lattices in the ℓ2 -norm. It does not by itself show that the reduction remains sound when the output
is required to have a prescribed cyclotomic module structure.
    Under the canonical embedding of a number field, ideals yield ideal lattices [LPR13a]. Higher-
rank modules similarly yield module lattices [LS15, FPMS22]. The ring action supports com-
pact representations and fast arithmetic [HPS98, LPR13b], underlies Ring-LWE and Module-
LWE [LPR13a, LS15], and enables specialized algorithms for module lattices [LPMSW19]. This
algebraic structure is no longer just a theoretical design choice. In 2024, NIST standardized
ML-KEM (FIPS 203), a module-lattice-based scheme derived from CRYSTALS–Kyber and based
on Module-LWE [BDK+ 18, Nat24b]. It also standardized ML-DSA (FIPS 204), derived from
CRYSTALS–Dilithium and relying on Module-LWE and a variant of Module-SIS [DKL+ 18, Nat24a].
    The cryptographic importance of module lattices has motivated broad work on structured lattices.
Prior work studies cyclic-lattice problems [Mic07], module unique-SVP and NTRU [FPMS22], proba-
bilistic reductions for approximation problems from arbitrary modules to free modules [DMMPMT23],
worst-case to average-case reductions for the approximate shortest independent vectors problem
(SIVP) at fixed module rank under the Extended Riemann Hypothesis [dBPTW26], and the geometry
of shortest vectors in random modules [GSVV25].
    For the Closest Vector Problem (CVP), a recent preprint proves that exact Euclidean decision-
CVP is NP-complete on coefficient lattices of nonzero principal ideals in power-of-two cyclotomic rings,
with a lift to principal cyclic ideal lattices [LFP26]. To the best of our knowledge, no deterministic
polynomial-time NP-hardness result for exact Euclidean SVP on full-rank free rank-two modules
over rings of integers in cyclotomic fields was known before the first version of this work appeared.

Notation. For a positive integer r, write [r] := {1, . . . , r}. For a prime q, let Fq denote the finite
field with q elements, and let F×  q := Fq \ {0} denote its multiplicative group. When an element of
Fq occurs as an exponent or in a binomial coefficient, we use its unique integer representative in

2.1     Euclidean lattices
A Euclidean lattice of rank n is a discrete additive subgroup L ⊆ Rm of the form

                                                   L = L(B) := BZn ,

where B ∈ Rm×n has linearly independent columns. These columns form a basis. The first minimum
of L is the length of a shortest nonzero lattice vector:

                                                   λ1 (L) = min ∥v∥2 .
                                                            0̸=v∈L

Definition 2.1 (SVP). The decision-SVP problem takes as input a basis B ∈ Qm×n with linearly
independent columns and a rational squared threshold ∆ ≥ 0, and asks whether

                                                     λ1 (L(B))2 ≤ ∆.

    The problem belongs to NP: a YES certificate is a nonzero integer coefficient vector for a short
lattice point. Standard cofactor bounds give a certificate of polynomial bit length, and exact rational
arithmetic checks both membership and the squared norm. See, e.g., [MG02]. The corresponding
search-SVP problem takes a full-column-rank basis B and outputs a shortest nonzero vector of
L(B).

2.3    X3C and Sidon sequences
An Exact Cover by 3-Sets (X3C) instance consists of a universe [M ] and three-element subsets
C1 , . . . , Cn ⊆ [M ]. Its incidence matrix A ∈ {0, 1}M ×n is defined by Aij = 1 when i ∈ Cj and
Aij = 0 otherwise. A selection vector ξ ∈ {0, 1}n specifies a choice of sets, with ξj = 1 exactly when
Cj is selected. We call ξ an X3C witness if

                                                           Aξ = 1M .

This equality says that every universe element belongs to exactly one selected set. The instance is
positive exactly when it has an X3C witness, which specifies an exact cover of the universe. X3C is
NP-complete [GJ79].
   An instance with M = 0 is positive. An instance whose incidence matrix has a zero row is
negative. Henceforth, assume M, n ≥ 1 and that every row of A is nonzero. Then M ≤ 3n.
   To index the n selection bits in the reduction, we choose distinct nonnegative integers whose
nonzero ordered differences are all distinct. Such a sequence is called a Sidon sequence. The following
lemma gives a deterministic construction with largest entry O(n2 ).

Lemma 2.3. For every n ≥ 1, one can deterministically compute integers

                               0 ≤ α1 < · · · < αn =: α⋆ ,                            α⋆ = O(n2 ),

such that all nonzero ordered differences αℓ − αj , j ̸= ℓ, are distinct. The computation takes time
polynomial in n.

Proof. Use the deterministic quadratic-residue Sidon construction of Erdős and Turán [ET41]. For
an explicit construction and the verification of the directed-difference property, see also Liu–Feng–
Pan [LFP26].

3         Reed–Solomon lattices and ideal cosets
Following the local-density approach in earlier SVP hardness reductions [Mic01, Mic12, BP23], we
use an ideal lattice obtained from a Construction A Reed–Solomon lattice. We require one fixed
ideal coset containing many short ring elements represented by weight-h binary coefficient vectors
x ∈ {0, 1}Fq , with [x] = a∈Fq xa ζ a ∈ OK . The first step of the reduction stores an X3C selection
                         ∑︁

vector ξ ∈ {0, 1}n in designated coordinates of x. Using Wan’s point-count estimates [Wan26c], we
show that for every ξ there are many such vectors x with [x] in the same fixed coset. At the same
time, the ideal is designed to have a minimum larger than the norms of these coset elements, so

5.4   Proof of the main theorem
Proof of Theorem 1.1. Every output of the reduction is a full-rank free OK -submodule of OK     2 and

hence has module rank two. As a lattice, each output has Z-rank 2(q − 1). For NP-hardness,
Theorem 5.2 gives the YES–NO equivalence for instances with M, n ≥ 1 and no zero row in A.
Together with the fixed YES and NO outputs in Section 5.3, this proves the correctness of the
reduction on all X3C instances. The reduction is deterministic, runs in polynomial time, and outputs
(q, m1 , m2 , S). The two module generators lie in OK  2 and have nonzero determinant.

    A certificate for a positive instance is a nonzero y ∈ OK     2 in power-basis coordinates with
                                                                            √
∥y∥2 ≤ S. By Lemma 2.2, every coefficient has absolute value at most S, so the certificate has
polynomial bit length. The verifier first checks that each ring coordinate of the input generators is
given by q − 1 integer coefficients and verifies the remaining input conditions in polynomial time. It
then solves y = z1 m1 + z2 m2 over K and checks that z1 , z2 ∈ OK to verify module membership.
Exact power-basis arithmetic performs this check and computes ∥y∥2 in polynomial time. Thus the
problem belongs to NP, completing the proof of Theorem 1.1.

Proof of Corollary 1.2. Given an X3C instance, apply the reduction from the proof of Theorem 1.1
to obtain (q, m1 , m2 , S), and query a search-SVP oracle on (q, m1 , m2 ). It returns a shortest
nonzero vector y ∈ M in power-basis coordinates. These coordinates have polynomial bit length
by Lemma 2.2 and ∥y∥2 ≤ ∥m1 ∥2 . Compute ∥y∥2 using Lemma 2.2. By the correctness of the
reduction, the X3C instance is positive exactly when ∥y∥2 ≤ S. Thus at most one oracle call decides
X3C in polynomial time, which proves the claimed Turing hardness.
